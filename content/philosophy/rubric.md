# rubric [roo-brik]

#### noun

1. a title, heading, direction, or the like, in a manuscript, book, statute, etc., written or printed in red or otherwise distinguished from the rest of the text.

2.a direction for the conduct of divine service or the administration of the sacraments, inserted in liturgical books.

3. any established mode of conduct or procedure; protocol.

4. an explanatory comment.

5. a class or category.

#### adjective

1.written, inscribed in, or marked with or as with red; rubrical.

2. red; ruddy.

## The plain idea

A **rubric** began life as a colour. The word comes from the Latin *rubrica*, "red earth" or red ochre, from *ruber*, "red" (the same root as *ruby*). Medieval scribes wrote headings, initial letters and instructions in red ink to make them stand out from the black text, and those red bits were called rubrics. Over the centuries the word drifted from **the red ink**, to **the heading written in it**, to **the instruction the heading gave**, to **any set of rules or categories** that organises something. Every meaning in the list above is a stop on that journey.

## Why it matters

A rubric is a frame that tells you how to read what is inside it. That is why I call philosophy the [rubric](philosophy.md) that envelopes art, religion, science, ethics and politics: philosophy is the red heading over everything else, the part that says what kind of thing you are looking at and how to judge it.

The word is also a small lesson in how language works. Nobody decided that "rubric" should mean "marking scheme". Each generation used it a little differently, and the meaning followed the use. [Wittgenstein](ludwigwittgenstein.md) would have smiled.

## In church

In liturgical books the rubrics are the instructions to the priest ("Here the people shall kneel"), printed in red, while the words to be spoken are in black. Hence the old clergy saying "*say the black, do the red*". It remains one of the best short descriptions of the difference between content and procedure you will find anywhere.

## In the classroom

Today most people meet the word in education, where a rubric is a **scoring guide**: a table listing the criteria for a piece of work and what each level of quality looks like.

### A worked example

A simple rubric for a short essay might look like this:

| Criterion | Excellent (3) | Adequate (2) | Weak (1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Argument | Clear claim, defended throughout | Claim present but drifts | No clear claim |
| Evidence | Specific, relevant, sourced | Some evidence, partly relevant | Assertions only |
| Clarity | Easy to follow, precise words | Mostly clear | Hard to follow |

An essay scoring 3, 2 and 3 gets 8 out of 9. The value is not the number. It is that the student can see *in advance* what "good" means, and the marker has to justify the grade criterion by criterion.

## In the age of AI

Rubrics have found an unexpected new job. When companies and researchers evaluate AI language models, they often can't check answers against a single correct one: there are many good ways to write a summary or explain a concept. So they write rubrics, lists of criteria such as "states the main finding", "cites a source", "makes no factual errors", and have human experts, or other AI models acting as judges, score responses against them. Some training methods now use rubric scores as the reward signal that teaches a model what a good answer looks like. The medieval scribe's red instruction has become, quite literally, the rulebook a machine learns from. The old problems come along too: a rubric rewards exactly what it names, so a clever student, or a clever model, can learn to tick the boxes without doing the thing the boxes were meant to capture.

## An everyday analogy: the recipe card

A recipe card in a kitchen is a rubric: the red heading (the dish), the instructions (what to do), and the criteria for success (golden brown, firm to the touch). It doesn't cook the meal, but it tells you how to recognise when you've cooked it well.

## Related words

- **Rubricate** – to mark in red, or to provide a text with headings.
- **Rubrication** – the red decoration in a manuscript.
- **Rubicund** – ruddy, red-faced; same Latin root.
- **Red-letter day** – from the saints' days printed in red in church calendars. Same idea, different word.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A rubric is just a marking scheme."** That is the newest meaning, not the original or only one.
- **"Rubric and Rubik are related."** No. The Rubik's cube is named after its Hungarian inventor, Ernő Rubik.
- **"Rubrics make judgment objective."** They make it *explicit*, which is valuable, but someone still had to choose the criteria.
