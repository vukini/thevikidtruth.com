## The plain idea

Physics tells you *how* to calculate. The philosophy of physics asks **what the calculation is telling you about the world.** Quantum mechanics, for example, predicts experimental results to more decimal places than any theory in history, and yet physicists still disagree about what a wavefunction *is*, whether measurement creates reality, and whether the universe splits. The equations are settled; their meaning is not. That gap is where the philosophy of physics lives.

## Why it matters

- **Interpretation is not optional.** Every physicist has a picture of what the maths describes, even if it is "shut up and calculate". Philosophy makes those pictures explicit and tests them for consistency.
- **It shapes research.** Einstein's relativity grew partly from philosophical worries about what "simultaneous" means. John Bell's 1964 theorem began as a philosophical question about hidden variables and became a lab experiment; the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics went to Aspect, Clauser and Zeilinger for testing it.
- **It guards against overselling.** Popular science is full of claims like "the observer creates reality" or "time is an illusion". The philosophy of physics asks what the theory actually supports.

## The big questions, briefly

- **The measurement problem.** Quantum states evolve smoothly by the Schrödinger equation, yet measurements give single definite outcomes. Major responses: Copenhagen-style views (the wavefunction is a tool for prediction), [Everett's many worlds](everettpicture.md) (every outcome happens in branching worlds), Bohmian mechanics (particles always have positions, guided by the wave), collapse theories such as GRW (the wave really collapses, spontaneously), and QBism (the wavefunction represents an agent's beliefs). Each solves the problem by giving something up.
- **Time.** The laws of physics run equally well forwards and backwards, yet we remember the past and not the future, and eggs break but don't unbreak. The usual answer points to entropy and an extraordinarily low-entropy start to the universe, the "past hypothesis". See [Past and Future](pastandfuture.md).
- **Probability.** What does a 50% chance mean in a deterministic theory like statistical mechanics, or in a many-worlds universe where every outcome happens? See [Physics and Chance](physicsandchance.md).
- **What exists.** Fields or particles? Is spacetime a thing or a set of relations? The "primitive ontology" approach insists a theory must say what stuff is in 3D space. See [Primitive Ontology](primitiveontology.md).
- **Symmetry.** Symmetries in physics (described by [groups](groups.md)) imply conservation laws, by Emmy Noether's 1918 theorem: symmetry in time gives conservation of energy. Why does nature care about symmetry at all?

## An everyday analogy: the satnav

Your satnav gets you home with perfect reliability. Does that mean the world is made of blue lines and robot voices? Obviously not; the satnav is a representation that works. Physics is an astonishingly good satnav. The philosophical question is how much of the map's structure belongs to the territory: are wavefunctions like the roads (real) or like the blue line (a useful overlay)?

## A worked example: Schrödinger's cat, four ways

A cat is in a box with a radioactive atom that has a 50% chance of decaying in an hour and triggering poison. Quantum mechanics describes the closed system as a superposition:

$$ |\Psi\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(|\text{decayed}\rangle|\text{dead}\rangle + |\text{intact}\rangle|\text{alive}\rangle\big) $$

- **Copenhagen-style:** \(|\Psi\rangle\) is a bookkeeping device; opening the box updates our predictions. Don't ask what the cat is "really" doing before.
- **Many worlds:** both branches are real; when you open the box, you split too, and each version of you sees one result.
- **Bohm:** the cat was always definitely alive or dead; the wavefunction guides the particles but our ignorance of exact positions makes it look random.
- **Collapse (GRW):** a cat contains so many particles that spontaneous collapse happens almost instantly; the superposition never really gets started at cat scale.

All four predict the same 50/50 outcome. They disagree about what the world is like. That is the philosophy of physics in one box.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Consciousness causes collapse."** A minority view (Wigner once flirted with it); most interpretations don't involve minds at all.
- **"Philosophy of physics is just philosophers who can't do physics."** Many of its leading figures, from Bell to David Albert and Sean Carroll, are or were physicists.
- **"These questions are untestable, so meaningless."** Bell's theorem was once called metaphysics. Collapse theories make predictions now being tested in labs.
