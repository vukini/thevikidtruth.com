# Epicurus (341–270 BCE) s

    b. Free Will and the slight swerve
   2. Image-flakes and perception

   6. Pleasure through moderation

## The plain idea

Epicurus taught that **the goal of life is pleasure**, and then spent his whole philosophy explaining that pleasure is not what you think. For Epicurus the highest pleasure is *ataraxia*, a calm, untroubled mind, and *aponia*, a body free of pain. Not feasting, but not being hungry. Not winning, but not being afraid. His recipe was simple living, good friends, and clear thinking to dissolve the fears that make people miserable.

He founded his school in a house with a garden in Athens around 306 BCE, so his followers were called "the Garden". Unusually for the time, it admitted women and slaves.

## Why it matters

Epicurus is one of the great ancestors of the modern scientific and secular worldview.

- **Atomism.** Following Democritus, he held that everything is made of tiny indivisible atoms moving in the void. No spirits, no divine hands pushing things around. His Roman follower Lucretius put the system into a long poem, *On the Nature of Things*; its rediscovery in 1417 helped spark Renaissance and early modern science (Stephen Greenblatt tells the story in *The Swerve*).
- **The swerve.** If atoms only fell in straight lines by fixed laws, everything would be determined. Epicurus added a tiny, uncaused "swerve" (*clinamen*) to leave room for free will. It is a strikingly early version of a question that physics and philosophy still argue about, including after quantum mechanics.
- **Ethics without fear.** His "four-part cure" (*tetrapharmakos*) is often summarised: *Don't fear god. Don't worry about death. What is good is easy to get. What is terrible is easy to endure.*

## The argument about death

This is his most famous and most useful argument:

> "Death is nothing to us. When we are, death is not; and when death is, we are not."

A worked version: being harmed requires a subject who experiences the harm. After death there is no subject (the atoms of the soul disperse). So death cannot harm the dead. What we fear is a scene in which we imagine ourselves lying in the grave, missing out, but we will not be there to miss anything. Lucretius added the "symmetry argument": you were not troubled by the eternity *before* you were born, so why be troubled by the one after?

Philosophers still debate whether this works. Thomas Nagel argued in "Death" (1970) that death can be bad as a *deprivation* of goods you would otherwise have had, even if nobody experiences the loss. But the argument remains a remarkably effective therapy for a fear most people never examine.

## The three kinds of desire

Epicurus sorted desires into:

1. **Natural and necessary** – food, shelter, friendship, peace of mind. Satisfy them; they are easy and cheap.
2. **Natural but not necessary** – fine food, sex, luxury. Fine in moderation, but don't depend on them.
3. **Neither natural nor necessary** – fame, power, wealth beyond need, immortality. These have no limit, so they can never be satisfied. Drop them.

## An everyday analogy: the hedonic treadmill

Modern psychology rediscovered Epicurus under the name *hedonic adaptation*: lottery winners and people who suffer serious accidents both tend to drift back toward their previous level of happiness. Buy the bigger car and in six months it is just "the car". Epicurus saw that chasing unlimited desires is a treadmill. His solution was not to run faster but to step off.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Epicurean means a lover of luxury and fine food."** Almost the opposite. Epicurus boasted he could be as happy as Zeus with bread and water, and asked a friend for "a little pot of cheese" so he could have a feast.
- **"He was an atheist."** He said gods exist but live in blissful indifference and take no interest in human affairs. In practice that removed divine punishment from the picture, which is why opponents called him an atheist.
- **"Hedonism means selfishness."** Epicurus valued friendship above almost everything: "Of all the things that wisdom provides for living one's entire life in happiness, the greatest by far is friendship."
