# Science

## The plain idea

Science is a **method for not fooling yourself**. Richard Feynman said it best: "The first principle is that you must not fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool." It is not a body of facts but a way of getting them: guess an explanation, work out what it predicts, test it against the world, and throw it away if the world disagrees, however much you like it.

## Why it matters

In about four centuries this method doubled human life expectancy, put people on the Moon, mapped the human genome and built the device you are reading this on. No other way of knowing has a track record remotely like it. It also matters because it is *self-correcting*: when science gets something wrong, the correction usually comes from other scientists using the same method.

## A very short history

People have studied nature for as long as there have been people: Babylonian astronomers recorded eclipses, Greek thinkers like [Thales](thalesofmiletos.md) sought natural explanations, and scholars in the Islamic world such as Ibn al-Haytham (around 1000 CE) insisted on controlled experiments in optics. What we call modern science took off in 17th-century Europe, when Galileo combined mathematics with experiment, Francis Bacon argued for systematic observation, and Newton showed that a few mathematical laws could explain both falling apples and planetary orbits. Scientific societies and journals followed, so results could be shared, checked and criticised.

The 20th century added two refinements. Karl Popper argued that what makes a claim scientific is that it is **falsifiable**: there must be some observation that would prove it wrong. And Thomas Kuhn showed that science does not only grow steadily but sometimes lurches through **paradigm shifts**, as when relativity and quantum mechanics replaced Newton's picture of the world at its edges. Today science is a global enterprise of millions of researchers, increasingly helped by computing and AI: the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went partly to the creators of AlphaFold, which predicts protein structures.

## An everyday analogy: the detective with a notebook

A good detective doesn't decide who did it and then collect only the evidence that fits. She lists suspects, asks what each theory predicts ("if it was the butler, there will be mud on his shoes"), checks, and crosses names off. Science is detective work with the whole universe as the crime scene and a strict rule that nobody, not even the detective, is above suspicion.

## A worked example: the scientific method on one question

**Question:** Does a new fertiliser make tomatoes grow bigger?

1. **Hypothesis:** plants given the fertiliser will produce heavier fruit.
2. **Prediction:** in a fair test, the treated group's average fruit weight will be higher.
3. **Design:** 40 plants of one variety, randomly assigned to two groups of 20 (randomising stops you unconsciously giving the best spots to the treated plants). Same soil, water and light. Ideally the person weighing doesn't know which group is which (blinding).
4. **Result:** treated average 152 g, untreated 140 g.
5. **Analysis:** is 12 g real or chance? A statistical test compares the difference with the natural spread between plants. If chance would rarely produce a gap this big, the result is *statistically significant*.
6. **Replication:** someone else repeats it in another greenhouse. Only results that survive replication become trusted knowledge.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Science proves things."** Mathematics proves; science *tests*. Theories become extremely well supported, never beyond revision.
- **"It's just a theory."** In science, a theory is a well-tested explanatory framework (evolution, relativity), not a hunch.
- **"Scientists all agree."** At the frontier they argue constantly. Consensus forms slowly, around evidence.
- **"If science changed its mind, it can't be trusted."** Changing its mind in response to evidence is exactly what makes it trustworthy. Recent years brought a useful reminder: the "replication crisis" in psychology and medicine showed many published findings didn't hold up, and the response has been better methods, such as pre-registered studies and open data.
