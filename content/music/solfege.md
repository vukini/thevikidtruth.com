# Solfege

```
 do di re ri mi fa fi so si la li ti do

 do ti te la le so se fa me me re ra do
```

```
 do di/ra re ri/me mi/fe fa fi/se so si/le l li/te ti/di do
```

```
di <-- do --> di

ra <-- re --> ri

me <-- mi

fe <-- fa --> fi

se <-- so --> si

te <-- ti
```

## The plain idea

**Solfège** gives each note of the scale a singable syllable: **do re mi fa so la ti do**. Instead of naming notes by letter (C, D, E), you name them by their *job* in the key. In "movable do" solfège, *do* is always the home note, whatever key you're in. In C major, do is C; in G major, do is G. The syllables above with changed vowels are the **chromatic** notes: going up, raised notes take an "i" (di, ri, fi, si, li); going down, lowered notes take an "e" or "a" (ra, me, se, le, te). So the chromatic scale up is do di re ri mi fa fi so si la li ti do, exactly as written at the top of this page.

## Why it matters

Solfège trains your ear to hear **function** rather than pitch. Every major-key melody sounds like a pattern of the same seven syllables, so once you can hear "that's mi–re–do" you can sing it, play it in any key, or write it down. It is how choirs learn to sight-sing and how many musicians develop "relative pitch", the skill of recognising notes in relation to each other. Absolute pitch (naming a note with no reference) is rare; relative pitch can be learned by almost anyone, and solfège is one of the best tools for it.

## A little history

The syllables come from an 11th-century hymn, *Ut queant laxis*, in which each line started a step higher. The monk Guido of Arezzo used the first syllable of each line, *ut re mi fa sol la*, to teach singers. *Ut* later became *do* (easier to sing), and *si* (from *Sancte Iohannes*) was added, which English-speaking teachers changed to *ti* so every syllable began with a different letter. *The Sound of Music*'s "Do-Re-Mi" made it famous worldwide.

## An everyday analogy: house numbers, not street names

Letter names are like street addresses: "the house at C Street". Solfège is like describing a house by its role: "the front door", "the kitchen", "the back door". Move to a new town (a new key) and the street names all change, but the front door is still the front door. Solfège describes the layout, which is what your ear actually hears.

## A worked example: singing a tune in solfège

"Mary Had a Little Lamb" begins:

> **mi re do re mi mi mi**, re re re, mi so so

Sing it in any key and the syllables are identical. Now notice what they teach: the tune centres on *do–re–mi*, steps down to *do* (home), and jumps up to *so* (the fifth), the most stable note after *do*. On guitar in C, that's E D C D E E E, D D D, E G G. In G, it's B A G A B B B, A A A, B D D. Different letters, same syllables, same tune.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Fixed do and movable do are the same."** In much of Europe and Latin America, *do* always means C (fixed do). Movable do, where *do* is the tonic, is the version described here and is better for ear training.
- **"Solfège is for singers only."** Instrumentalists who sing their lines internally tend to phrase better and learn by ear faster.
- **"There is only one way to do minor keys."** Two systems exist: "la-based minor" (the minor tonic is *la*) and "do-based minor" (the minor tonic is *do*, with me, le and te). Teachers argue about it; pick one and be consistent.
