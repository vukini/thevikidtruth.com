## The plain idea

A **chord progression** is the sequence of chords under a song, the harmonic road the melody travels on. The Roman numerals above describe progressions in any key: in C major, I–IV–V is C–F–G; in G it's G–C–D. Learn a progression once as numbers and you can play it in every key.

A handful of progressions carry an astonishing share of popular music:

| Progression | In C | In G | Sound and examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| I–IV–V | C F G | G C D | the backbone of blues, rock'n'roll, folk ("La Bamba", "Twist and Shout") |
| I–V–vi–IV | C G Am F | G D Em C | the modern pop progression ("Let It Be", "With or Without You", "Someone Like You") |
| vi–IV–I–V | Am F C G | Em C G D | the same chords, starting on minor: moodier |
| I–vi–IV–V | C Am F G | G Em C D | the 1950s "doo-wop" progression ("Stand by Me", "Every Breath You Take") |
| ii–V–I | Dm G C | Am D G | the heart of jazz |
| 12-bar blues | C C C C / F F C C / G F C G | | the form underneath blues and early rock |

## Why it matters

Progressions are the shortcut to playing hundreds of songs. When you recognise "that's just I–V–vi–IV", a new song is 80% learned before you start. They also make writing songs much less mysterious: choose a progression, play it on a loop, and hum melodies over it until one sticks. That is how a great many songs are actually written.

## An everyday analogy: a journey with landmarks

Each chord has a role, a bit like stops on a commute. **I** is home. **IV** is a pleasant detour that moves you away. **V** is the last stop before home, full of anticipation. **vi** is home's shadowy twin, the relative minor, sharing two notes with I. A progression is a route between these stops, and the satisfying ones end with V→I, the train pulling into home station.

## A worked example: the 12-bar blues in A

One bar per chord (four beats each), with the dominant 7th chords that give the blues its flavour:

```
| A7  | A7  | A7  | A7  |
| D7  | D7  | A7  | A7  |
| E7  | D7  | A7  | E7  |
```

That's I–I–I–I / IV–IV–I–I / V–IV–I–V. The final E7 (the "turnaround") sends you back to the top. Play it with a shuffle rhythm and improvise over it using the A minor pentatonic scale (A C D E G) and you are playing the blues. This exact form underlies Robert Johnson, Chuck Berry's "Johnny B. Goode", and Elvis's "Hound Dog".

## The minor progressions above, decoded

- **i–iv–v** and **i–iv–V**: in A minor, Am–Dm–Em or Am–Dm–E. The major V (E, borrowed from the harmonic minor scale) gives a much stronger pull home, which is why classical minor pieces almost always use it.
- **i–VI–III–VII**: in A minor, Am–F–C–G. The same four chords as the pop progression, starting on the minor; epic and anthemic.
- **ii–V–I** and its minor form **ii°–V–i** are the building blocks of jazz standards.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Using a common progression is unoriginal."** Melody, rhythm, lyrics and arrangement make a song distinctive. Thousands of great songs share four chords.
- **"Progressions must start on I."** Starting on vi, IV or ii changes the mood completely.
- **"You need all the chords of the key."** Plenty of hits use just two or three.
