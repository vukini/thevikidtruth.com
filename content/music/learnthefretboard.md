# Learning the Fretboard

## The Jazz Method for Learning the Fretboard

1. Know the Major Scale
1. Learn String by String
1. Play a chord then improvise on a single string. (C chord for example)
1. Then do with 2 strings at a time
1. Then change to simple other keys
1. Improvise blues on one string
1. Play tunes on one string
1. Then do on two adjacent strings
1. Then two non adjacent strings

## The plain idea

The guitar fretboard looks like a grid of six strings and twenty-odd frets, over 120 positions in all, but it hides a simple logic: **each fret is one semitone, and the notes repeat every twelve frets.** Learn the notes on one string, learn how the strings relate, and the whole board unlocks. The jazz method above does it the musical way: one string at a time, always improvising, so you learn the notes by *using* them rather than memorising a chart.

## Why it matters

If you only know chord shapes, you're reading phrases without knowing the alphabet. Knowing where the notes are lets you find any chord or scale anywhere, transpose songs instantly, play melodies by ear, and improvise with intent instead of patterns.

## The basic facts

- **Open strings**, low to high: **E A D G B E** ("Eddie Ate Dynamite, Good Bye Eddie").
- Each string is a fourth (5 frets) above the one below, **except G to B**, which is a major third (4 frets). That one exception is why chord and scale shapes shift by a fret when they cross onto the B string.
- **Natural notes** are two frets apart, except **B–C** and **E–F**, which are one fret apart.
- The **12th fret** repeats the open string an octave up; the dots on fret markers (3, 5, 7, 9, 12) are your landmarks.

## A worked example: finding every C on the neck

1. Low E string: E (0) F (1) G (3) A (5) B (7) **C (8)**.
2. A string: A (0) B (2) **C (3)**.
3. D string: D (0) E (2) F (3) G (5) A (7) B (9) **C (10)**.
4. G string: G (0) A (2) B (4) **C (5)**.
5. B string: **C (1)**.
6. High E: same as low E, **C (8)**.

Now notice the **octave shapes**: from C on the A string (fret 3), the C two strings up on the G string is two frets higher (fret 5). From C on the low E (fret 8), the octave on the D string is at fret 10, again two frets up and two strings over. Learn these two or three shapes and any note you know on the low strings gives you its octaves everywhere. Repeat with a different note each day.

## An everyday analogy: learning a city

You don't learn a city by memorising a map of every street. You learn one main road, walk it often, then learn the roads that cross it, then the neighbourhoods. The method above does the same: one string (a main road), then two adjacent strings (a neighbourhood), then non-adjacent strings (shortcuts across town).

## Practising the method

- **Say the note names aloud** as you play. It feels slow; it's what makes the knowledge stick.
- **Play over a drone or backing track** in one key, so every note has a musical meaning.
- **Use one string for a whole tune**: playing "Happy Birthday" on just the B string forces you to find every note by ear and by name.
- **A few minutes a day** beats an hour once a week.

## Common misconceptions

- **"I'll learn the notes eventually just by playing."** Many players gig for years without knowing them. It takes deliberate practice.
- **"You need to memorise the whole chart at once."** Start with the natural notes on the low E and A strings; they are the root notes of most chord shapes.
- **"Tab makes notes unnecessary."** Tab tells you where; note names tell you why.
