# Arabic: Start Here

## The plain idea

Arabic is a Semitic language, related to Hebrew and Aramaic, spoken as a first language by somewhere around 300–400 million people across the Middle East and North Africa, and the liturgical language of Islam for well over a billion more. It is written right to left in an alphabet of **28 letters**, mostly consonants; short vowels are usually left unwritten and are supplied by the reader.

Two facts make Arabic different from most languages a learner meets:

1. **Diglossia.** There is a formal written standard, **Modern Standard Arabic** (MSA, *al-fuṣḥā*), used in news, books and speeches, and a family of spoken dialects, Egyptian, Levantine, Gulf (including the Emirati Arabic I'm learning here), Iraqi, Maghrebi and more, used in daily life. They differ about as much as Italian, Spanish and Latin, and nobody speaks MSA at home.
2. **Roots and patterns.** Most words are built from a root of three consonants carrying a core meaning, poured into vowel patterns.

## Why it matters

Arabic is an official language in more than 20 countries and one of the six official languages of the United Nations. Historically it carried the science of the medieval world: the words *algebra* (*al-jabr*), *algorithm* (from the mathematician al-Khwarizmi), *alchemy*, *zero* and *coffee* all came into English through Arabic. And for anyone living in the Gulf, even a little Arabic transforms everyday encounters.

## 1. The alphabet and pronunciation

Arabic is written **right to left** in a joined-up script of 28 letters. Most letters have up to four shapes (alone, start, middle, end of a word), and short vowels are normally not written, only long ones (ا ā, و ū, ي ī).

| Letter | Name | Sound | Letter | Name | Sound |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ا | alif | ā | ب | bāʾ | b |
| ت | tāʾ | t | ث | thāʾ | th (think) |
| ج | jīm | j | ح | ḥāʾ | breathy h |
| خ | khāʾ | kh (loch) | د | dāl | d |
| ذ | dhāl | dh (this) | ر | rāʾ | rolled r |
| ز | zāy | z | س | sīn | s |
| ش | shīn | sh | ص | ṣād | heavy s |
| ض | ḍād | heavy d | ط | ṭāʾ | heavy t |
| ظ | ẓāʾ | heavy dh | ع | ʿayn | throat squeeze |
| غ | ghayn | gargled gh | ف | fāʾ | f |
| ق | qāf | deep k | ك | kāf | k |
| ل | lām | l | م | mīm | m |
| ن | nūn | n | ه | hāʾ | h |
| و | wāw | w / ū | ي | yāʾ | y / ī |

Plus ء (hamza, the glottal stop) and ة (tāʾ marbūṭa, the feminine ending *-a*).

## 2. Core grammar

- **The article** is *al-* (ال), attached to the word: *kitāb* a book, *al-kitāb* the book. Before "sun letters" (t, d, r, s, sh, n...) the *l* assimilates: *ash-shams* (the sun), *ar-rajul* (the man).
- **Gender**: nouns are masculine or feminine; most feminines end in ة *-a*: *mudarris* (male teacher), *mudarrisa* (female teacher).
- **No "is" in the present**: *al-bayt kabīr* (البيت كبير), "the house (is) big". *anā min Dubay*, "I (am) from Dubai".
- **Pronouns**: *anā* I, *anta / anti* you (m/f), *huwa* he, *hiya* she, *naḥnu* we, *antum* you all, *hum* they.
- **Possession** with endings: *kitābī* my book, *kitābak / kitābik* your book (m/f), *kitābuh* his book.
- **Roots and patterns** (below) build most of the vocabulary.

## A worked example: one root, many words

Take the root **k-t-b**, which has to do with writing:

| Arabic | Pattern | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *kataba* | C**a**C**a**C**a** | he wrote |
| *kitāb* | C**i**C**ā**C | book |
| *kātib* | C**ā**C**i**C | writer |
| *maktab* | **ma**CC**a**C | office, desk (place of writing) |
| *maktaba* | **ma**CC**a**C**a** | library, bookshop |
| *maktūb* | **ma**CC**ū**C | written; also "fate" (what is written) |

Learn a root and a dozen patterns, and you can often guess words you've never seen. It's a bit like Lego again: fixed bricks (the consonants), interchangeable connectors (the vowel patterns).

## Arabizi: Arabic in Latin letters

When people began texting on phones without Arabic keyboards, they invented **Arabizi** (Arabic + *Englizi*), writing Arabic with Latin letters and using **numbers for sounds English lacks**, chosen because the digit looks like the Arabic letter. That is what my [Arabizi page](arabizi.md) records:

| Number | Arabic letter | Sound |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | ء hamza | the glottal stop, the catch in "uh-oh" |
| 3 | ع ʿayn | a voiced squeeze deep in the throat |
| 3' (or gh) | غ ghayn | a gargled "gh", like a French r |
| 5 (or kh) | خ khāʾ | the "ch" of Scottish *loch* |
| 6 | ط ṭāʾ | an emphatic, heavy t |
| 6' | ظ ẓāʾ | an emphatic dh/z |
| 7 | ح ḥāʾ | a breathy h from the throat |
| 8 | ق qāf | a q pronounced far back |
| 9 | ص ṣād | an emphatic, heavy s |

So *3arabi* is *ʿarabī*, "Arabic", and *ma3a salama* is "goodbye". Arabizi is informal, but it is everywhere in Gulf messaging, and it's a great bridge while you learn the real script.

## An everyday analogy: texting shorthand

Arabizi is the Arabic equivalent of writing "gr8" or "l8r" in English: a playful, practical hack born from keyboard limits that became a culture of its own.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Arabic is one language everyone speaks the same way."** The dialects differ substantially; a Moroccan and an Iraqi may switch to MSA or a neutral style to understand each other.
- **"The script is impossibly hard."** Twenty-eight letters, most with up to four joined forms, can be learned in a few weeks of steady practice.
- **"Arabizi is 'wrong' Arabic."** It's a different spelling system for the same spoken language, not a different language.
