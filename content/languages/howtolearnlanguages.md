# Learning Methods: How to Learn Languages

1. **Comprehensible input** - We learn by "understanding messages" - See Krashen
1. **Read, Listen & Watch** in target language. Mags, music, shows, youtube, books, etc...
1. **Speak with real people** as often as you can
1. **A little bit every day**, every time
1. **Extensive input over intensive** - listen passively
1. *Examples:*

## The plain idea

The list above comes down to one idea from the linguist **Stephen Krashen**: we *acquire* language by understanding messages, not by memorising rules. His "input hypothesis" (1980s) says we progress when we hear or read things we *almost* fully understand, a level he called **i+1**: what you already know, plus a little more. Grammar study helps you notice and polish, but the engine is lots of understandable input, followed by real conversation.

## Why it matters

Most of us spent years in school language classes and came out unable to order coffee. The reason is usually not talent but method: a few hours a week of drills and tests, very little meaningful input. Language is a skill, like swimming. Reading about strokes helps a bit; time in the water is what actually works.

## What the research supports

- **Input is essential.** Learners with more exposure to understandable material progress faster; that's well established.
- **Output and interaction matter too.** Merrill Swain's "output hypothesis" and Michael Long's work on interaction showed that speaking, getting stuck and being corrected forces you to notice gaps that listening alone hides. So the "speak with real people" step on my list is not optional.
- **Spaced repetition works for vocabulary.** Reviewing a word just as you're about to forget it (the principle behind flashcard apps like Anki) is one of the best-supported findings in learning science.
- **Little and often beats cramming.** Distributed practice wins over massed practice, in languages as everywhere else.
- **AI is a new tool.** Since the mid-2020s, AI chat and voice tutors let you practise conversation at any hour and get instant explanations. They're excellent for low-stakes practice, but they still make mistakes, so check anything important with a native speaker or good reference.

## An everyday analogy: a child at the dinner table

A toddler doesn't study the past tense. She hears "we went to the park" a thousand times, at the dinner table, in stories, in songs, always attached to meaning. Then one day she says "we goed", proof that she's extracting the pattern herself, and soon after, "we went". Adults learn faster than toddlers in many ways, but we skip the thousand hours of meaningful input and wonder why it doesn't stick.

## A worked example: a 30-minute daily routine

1. **10 minutes of input** – a podcast, a show with target-language subtitles, or a graded reader at a level where you understand about 90–95% of the words.
2. **5 minutes of review** – spaced-repetition flashcards of words you met *in that input* (not from random lists), with the full sentence.
3. **10 minutes of output** – write a few sentences about your day (as I do on my [Spanish](spanish.md) and [Swahili](swahili.md) pages), or speak with a tutor, a language exchange partner or an AI tutor.
4. **5 minutes of noticing** – look up one grammar point that confused you today.

Thirty minutes a day is over 180 hours a year. The US Foreign Service Institute estimates roughly 600 to 750 hours of class time for English speakers to reach professional working proficiency in the closest languages (Spanish, French, Italian), and far more for Arabic or Japanese, so a steady daily habit gets you to real conversations within a year and to fluency in a few.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Adults can't learn languages well."** Adults often learn faster than children at first; accent is harder after childhood, but fluency is very achievable.
- **"You need to be in the country."** Immersion helps, but plenty of people abroad stay in an English bubble, and plenty at home reach fluency through daily input and online conversation.
- **"Passive listening alone is enough."** Background audio you don't understand does little. Input must be *comprehensible*.
- **"Some people just don't have the gift."** Aptitude varies, but time and method explain most of the difference.
