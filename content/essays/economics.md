# Economics

Economics is the study of how humans (or any intelligent agent) interact in
an **environment** of **scarce resources**.

## The plain idea

Economics starts from one brute fact: **we want more than there is.** Time, money, land, attention, oil and talent are all limited, so every choice to use them one way is a choice not to use them another way. Economics studies how people, firms and governments make those choices, and what happens when millions of choices collide in markets.

The single most useful idea in the subject is **opportunity cost**: the true cost of anything is the best alternative you gave up to get it. An evening watching a film costs the ticket price *plus* whatever else you would have done with the evening.

## Why it matters

Economic ideas shape the price of your groceries, the interest on your mortgage, whether your job exists, and how governments respond to crises. The difference between the Great Depression of the 1930s and the faster recovery after 2008 owed a lot to economists having learned from the first one. Understanding a few core ideas protects you from both political slogans and financial scams.

## The two halves

- **Microeconomics** studies individual decisions and particular markets: why the price of eggs rises after a bird-flu outbreak, how a monopoly behaves, what a minimum wage does to employment.
- **Macroeconomics** studies the economy as a whole: growth, unemployment, inflation, interest rates, government debt and the business cycle.

## Supply and demand: a worked example

Suppose that at a price \(P\) (in dollars), coffee drinkers in a town want \(Q_d = 100 - 10P\) cups a day, and cafés are willing to sell \(Q_s = 20 + 10P\).

The market settles where the two are equal:

$$ 100 - 10P = 20 + 10P \;\Rightarrow\; 20P = 80 \;\Rightarrow\; P = 4,\quad Q = 60 $$

So 60 cups a day at \$4. Now a frost raises bean costs and cafés will only supply \(Q_s = 10P\) at each price. The new equilibrium: \(100 - 10P = 10P\), so \(P = 5\) and \(Q = 50\). The price rises by a dollar and ten fewer cups are drunk. Nobody planned that adjustment; it emerged from buyers and sellers each reacting to prices. That is the "invisible hand" Adam Smith described in 1776, in one line of algebra.

## An everyday analogy: the price as a messenger

Friedrich Hayek's insight (1945) was that a price is a compressed message. When copper gets scarce, nobody needs to know *why*, whether a mine flooded or demand for electric cars rose. The higher price alone tells users to economise and producers to dig more. A market is a vast information-processing machine, and prices are its signals.

## Big schools of thought

- **Classical** (Smith, Ricardo): markets, specialisation, and the gains from trade.
- **Keynesian** (Keynes, 1936): in a slump, spending can collapse, and government spending can help restore demand.
- **Monetarist** (Friedman): inflation is "always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon"; control the money supply.
- **Austrian** (Mises, Hayek): emphasise prices as information, time preference and the dangers of central planning and cheap credit. See [Time Preference Theory](timepreference.md).
- **Behavioural** (Kahneman, Thaler): real people are not perfectly rational; they fear losses more than they value gains and procrastinate predictably.

Modern mainstream economics is a blend, and the last few years have tested it hard: the inflation surge of 2021–2023, the fastest interest-rate rises in decades, and renewed debates about tariffs and trade.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Trade is a zero-sum game."** Voluntary trade usually benefits both sides; that is why it happens. The distribution of the gains can still be very unequal.
- **"Printing money makes everyone richer."** It can help in a slump, but beyond the economy's capacity it mostly raises prices.
- **"Economics is about money."** It is about choices under scarcity. Money is just the most convenient measuring stick.
- **"Economists agree on nothing."** They argue loudly at the frontier but agree on a lot: rent controls tend to shrink housing supply, tariffs are paid largely by domestic buyers, and so on.
