Politics is the study of how Humans (and other intelligent agents) self-organize
to govern themselves.

## The plain idea

Wherever people live together they have to settle three questions: **who decides, how they decide, and what happens to those who disagree.** Politics is the business of answering those questions, and political science and political philosophy are the study of how it is done and how it ought to be done. Harold Lasswell's classic short definition: politics is "who gets what, when, how".

## Why it matters

Politics decides the rules you live under: what you pay in tax, what you may say, who goes to war, whether the water is clean. You can ignore politics, as the old line goes, but it won't ignore you. Understanding how it works is the difference between being a participant and being a pawn.

## The forms of government

Aristotle sorted constitutions by **how many rule** (one, few, or many) and **in whose interest** (the common good or their own). Rule by one is monarchy at its best and tyranny at its worst; by a few, aristocracy or oligarchy; by the many, what he called "polity" or, in its corrupt form, democracy (by which he meant mob rule). The labels have shifted, but the two axes still work. Modern regimes add a third question: are the rulers **constrained**, by elections, courts, a free press and the rule of law, or not? That constraint, more than voting alone, is what separates a liberal democracy from an elected autocracy.

## The big questions

- **Legitimacy.** Why should anyone obey a government? Thomas Hobbes (1651) said: because without one, life is "nasty, brutish, and short", so we accept a sovereign for safety. John Locke (1689) said government is legitimate only with the consent of the governed and only while it protects life, liberty and property. Rousseau spoke of the "general will". These social contract theories still underpin modern constitutions.
- **Liberty versus equality.** How much should the state redistribute? Classical liberals and libertarians (Hayek, Nozick) stress individual freedom and markets; social democrats and egalitarians (Rawls) stress fairness and a safety net. Rawls's thought experiment: design society from behind a "veil of ignorance", not knowing whether you'll be born rich or poor.
- **Change versus tradition.** Edmund Burke argued that institutions embody the accumulated wisdom of generations and should be reformed cautiously; radicals argue unjust institutions should be torn down. This is the root of the left–right divide. See [What is Conservatism](whatisconservatism.md).
- **Power and its limits.** Montesquieu's separation of powers (legislature, executive, judiciary) and the rule of law exist because, as Lord Acton put it, "power tends to corrupt, and absolute power corrupts absolutely."

## An everyday analogy: the shared flat

Five flatmates share a kitchen. Who buys the washing-up liquid? Is it a rota, a vote, the person who cares most, or the loudest? Can someone who never cleans be thrown out? Do the rules apply to the person who pays the most rent? Every one of these is a political question in miniature, and every system of government is an answer scaled up to millions of people.

## A worked example: how voting rules change outcomes

Twenty-one voters choose between three candidates:

| Voters | 1st choice | 2nd | 3rd |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | A | B | C |
| 7 | B | C | A |
| 6 | C | B | A |

- **First past the post:** A wins with 8 votes, even though 13 of 21 voters rank A *last*.
- **Instant runoff:** C has fewest first preferences and is eliminated; those 6 votes go to B, who now has 13 and wins.
- **Head-to-head (Condorcet):** B beats A 13–8 and beats C 15–6. B is the candidate preferred to every rival.

Same voters, same preferences, different winners. Kenneth Arrow proved in 1951 that no ranked voting system can satisfy every reasonable fairness condition at once. Choosing the rules is itself a political act.

## Politics today

Recent decades have brought strong currents worth understanding whatever your own views: the rise of populist movements on both left and right, deepening polarisation in many democracies, declining trust in institutions, and the effect of social media on how political information spreads. Organisations such as V-Dem and Freedom House have reported a long-running decline in democratic quality across much of the world since the mid-2000s, though the picture differs strongly from country to country.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Politics is just what politicians do."** Families, workplaces and online communities all have politics.
- **"Left and right are fixed."** Their meanings shift across countries and decades; free trade, for instance, has moved between sides.
- **"Democracy means majority rule, full stop."** Liberal democracy also protects minorities and individual rights from majorities, through constitutions and courts.
