# An Introduction to the Language of Category Theory by Steven Roman

### About the book

*An Introduction to the Language of Category Theory* by **Steven Roman** (Springer, 2017) is a compact, student-friendly textbook on category theory. It covers the core vocabulary of the subject, categories, functors, natural transformations, universal properties, limits and colimits, adjoints, and the Yoneda lemma, with plenty of examples and exercises (many with solutions), aimed at advanced undergraduates and beginning graduate students who have some abstract algebra behind them.

### What category theory is, and why it is worth the effort

Category theory is sometimes called "the mathematics of mathematics". Instead of studying objects by looking *inside* them (what a group contains, what a set holds), it studies them by the **arrows between them**, the structure-preserving maps, and asks what can be said purely from how things connect. That shift in viewpoint turns out to reveal the same patterns recurring across algebra, topology, logic and computer science, which is why it has become a common language linking fields that once looked unrelated.

For a programmer this is more than abstraction for its own sake. Category theory underlies a great deal of functional programming: functors, monads and the rest in languages like [Haskell](haskell.md) come straight from here, and the "universal property" way of thinking, define a thing by the unique way everything else maps to it, is a powerful design idea in its own right.

### An everyday analogy

Studying a city by cataloguing every building is one approach; studying it by the roads, who connects to whom, and how traffic must flow, is another. Category theory is the second approach applied to mathematics: it learns about objects almost entirely from the pattern of roads (arrows) between them, and discovers that very different cities often share the same road map.

### Why I keep this page

Category theory has a fearsome reputation, and Roman's book is one of the gentler on-ramps. The links above collect the book's first chapter plus several free introductions (Simmons, Peter Smith's "Gentle Introduction") so a curious reader can sample the subject before committing.
