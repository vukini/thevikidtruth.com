## Where the claim comes from

Copper is everywhere electricity goes: house wiring, motors, transformers, data centres. It's also one of the metals I track every day on the [Markets board](/markets/), because cable makers live and die by its price. So it's easy to assume it must be the best conductor there is.

## What's actually true

- **Copper is the yardstick.** In 1913 the International Electrotechnical Commission adopted the **International Annealed Copper Standard (IACS)**. Annealed copper at 20 °C is defined as **100% IACS** (58 MS/m), and every other metal is rated against it. Modern high-conductivity copper routinely reaches 101–102% IACS.
- **Silver beats it.** Pure silver is about **106% IACS**, the most conductive metal. It isn't used for ordinary wiring because it's expensive and mechanically weak, so it stays in specialist uses like contacts.
- **Gold is worse, not better.** Gold is about **70% IACS**. It's used on connectors because it doesn't corrode, not because it conducts well.
- **Aluminium is the other big one.** Pure aluminium is about **61% IACS**, but it's much lighter and cheaper per amp carried. That's why overhead transmission lines are mostly aluminium conductor, while copper dominates cables, buildings and machines.

## The truth

**The truth:** Copper isn't the best conductor. It's the best conductor you can afford by the tonne, and that's why its price is a barometer for the whole electrical industry. When copper gets expensive, engineers reach for aluminium, never for silver.
